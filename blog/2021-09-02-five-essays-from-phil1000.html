prompt: what make you, you?

A multitude of factors contribute to who someone will be, and everyone is essentially a test subject for the environment. Aside from primal instincts, individual human behavior comes from personal experience and learned behaviors. As people age, they gain skills through life experiences that shape their future decisions, allowing the mind to be an everchanging aspect of this world. Things such as gender, religion, race, culture, socioeconomic status, family, geography, hobbies, and genes all play a part in who someone can and will be. In addition to this, luck and randomness also play a larger part than some may think. Failure and learning from one’s mistakes are also key to growing. Someone that receives no exposure to the outside world or has limited resources will also seem different than the rest of the general population for an area. An interesting thing to note is that some factors are chosen, and some are not.

An example of a factor one cannot choose would be their parents. Whom someone’s parents play a huge role in whom they will become as a person, as they are the people that teach an individual the basis of everything they first learn. A person is essentially a biological mashup of their parents and will behave in similar ways to them both consciously and subconsciously throughout their life. Things like IQ are genetic as well, which will decide in a way the types of intelligent decisions someone will make later. The main difference between an individual’s parents and factors such as what they like to do or preferences is that you cannot choose who your parents are, but you can decide what you like and are interested in.

When it comes to factors that someone can choose, (but are likely influenced by other life experiences) things such as hobbies, general preferences, clothes that they wear, and many other aspects are at hand. Past experiences as well and a bit of randomness likely contribute the most to the active decisions that people make day in and day out. Our lives are filled with choices and the choices that one decides to make trigger a domino effect of future decisions that will eventually happen. If one chooses to not get up for the first day of a new job it could potentially cause them to miss out on an interaction that opens the door for an entirely different set of events that would dictate the future pillars of their life. Choosing to act on an opportunity makes all the difference when thinking about the future.

The last main aspect that determines who someone is today would be randomness, luck, accidents, and failure. Some of the best realizations people have, happen by accident or as a result of a random act that happened. This can go both ways, as there is always good and bad luck. The simple act of leaving your house 5 minutes later in the morning could make the difference between being in a life-threatening car crash and ultimately dying. Everything in life is a risk of varying degrees and sometimes a random variable that a person cannot control is the best for falling into or getting out of a situation.

In conclusion, no one human is simple enough by nature to be completely described by a set of factors. But if it must be broken down, then there are chosen factors, non-chosen factors, and completely random factors that all contribute to any given person’s experiences and present/future choices. Positive interactions with other people open doors and can shape who you are as an individual.

prompt: what is the best proof for the existence of god?

The best proof for the existence of God would be to observe the arguments of Pantheism. Pantheism essentially states that the higher power of the universe encompasses all aspects of anything in the universe. This means that every living (or dead) person, thing, law, object, and idea is itself a part of the universe, and therefore is “god.” Typical religions in the modern-day (and historically) have often clashed with each other over ideas, origin stories, and land. Pantheism ignores all of this because it is not focused on a specific prophet or sacred text that was passed down through generations to prove its validity. Abrahamic religions all have their legend for how their history went down, and study slightly different texts that cause them to clash as a result. In addition to this, I would argue that Pantheism is also the most logical (or likely) religion for the sake of science.

Within Pantheism, the God themselves in question is essentially the mash of all things in existence, meaning that anything is living proof. Since the proof for a God within Pantheism is so broad, one needs to consider the reasons why Pantheism is superior to the next religion. As stated above, typical religion often idolizes either a singular god/figure or a few. Almost all religions have some sort of sacred text and following as well. Typical religions also often provide a view of the afterlife for those that abide by the rules of the texts. In my opinion, humans were not put on Earth to pray to a God that we likely made up. In ancient times, most people likely did not know better and followed the word of the church because they were scared to do otherwise. Religion often corrupts governments and can hold science back to a point where humans are evolving backward. Another flaw with typical religions is that there are so many different ones that swear they are the one true religion. Pantheism also bypasses this because there is no central say, rule, or law that says what people must do or believe in. I see Pantheism as almost a negative religion in the sense that it doesn’t care about the typical intricacies of modern religion. If true, Pantheism includes all religions as well and doesn’t even try to invalidate them. If someone else’s validation is a part of another religion, then it is still a part of Pantheism and the universe.

Pantheism could be compared to Atheism in the sense that there is no humanoid God to follow, but I would say that Atheists believe in nothing at all, especially not believing in the chance for some higher power to exist in the first place. Some may be turned away by Pantheism because there is no historical text or central figure or even a large following, but this is what makes it different. And if I had to pick a religion to follow, it would be Pantheism. Pantheism is logical for the pure fact that nothing science perceives as true challenges its definition. The Pantheist takes the world for what it is and embraces all aspects of it as a part of the whole that is their God. Pantheism does not break the laws of physics or actively try to invalidate others’ views. Pantheism is a step back that almost looks at all religions and takes any action as evidence for its existence.

prompt: is experience universal?

To start, we need to determine what it means to agree on something existing. Existence is, “the fact or state of living or having objective reality.” Or under philosophical terms, “the ontological property of being.” With this established, we now know that for something to exist, it must be universally perceived the same way (in the sense of agreeing something exists visually). I would also like to note the difference between acknowledging existence (objectively) and having an opinion on something that exists (subjective). Both can exist and everyone experiences “objective” things in their manner. This leads me to the question of this debate: How can we move from subjective claims of experience to objective claims of “fact”?

For one’s subjective claim to have any value in contributing to what may become a fact, it needs validity. I would argue that no “fact” can be universally trusted off one person’s account and validity can only be accomplished through multiple accounts of the same conclusion (or at least close enough to the same conclusion). Think about the entire ideology behind group decisions or councils of people deciding what is best for themselves. Often, time groups that hold more accounts overall conclude more accurate things (peer-reviewed science or democratic systems) However, this can also work oppositely, in the sense of Religion or corrupt politics, but eventually breaks down as “wrong” ideas fall out of favor over time (Communism). So, for subjective accounts to be taken as fact, multiple people need to all agree on the definition.

Humans are notoriously good at being incredibly wrong, and we often change what we agree on as fact. Also, a fact is just something that has enough evidence / where enough people agree on it. It is also important that humans challenge facts to ensure their validity through time (Scientific Method). Only after evidence is provided, in the forms of experiments, experience, and multiple accounts we can then conclude objective fact. And even after this, we need to remain skeptical of our findings. I would argue that humans cannot evolve forward without the right amount of skepticism about what we agree on as the truth. What I experience about something determined as “fact” will undoubtedly be different than the next guys. And it will especially be different when compared to someone that has more knowledge about a subject than I do. Therefore, we must take multiple experiences into account when determining truth.

This begs the question: Does anyone individual experience things the same way as anyone else? I would say that the answer is yes and no. The answer is yes because as stated earlier if something exists then it is perceived the same way objectively (visually and the fact that it is in the physical realm). But beyond initial perception, the answer of yes breaks down because everyone will have their own opinion on said item. Subjectiveness takes over and deviation for the previously agreed upon truth is slightly broken. The consensus will likely stay the same, but humans will always differ slightly. And if everyone perceived it in the same way forever, then there couldn’t have been skepticism in the first place to create/test new truths. So, to conclude, “Fact” can only be determined from a multitude of various accounts (subjectively) of an objective instance to cover any ground. Once something is agreed upon, it should frequently be tested or challenged, to ensure validity and agreement between most. It is almost impossible for everyone to agree but with checks and balances, we can get close.

prompt: why would a benevolent god allow suffering?

A benevolent God simply would not allow suffering in the world. I would argue that if one truly existed, suffering would not exist either and only joy would be felt by all things. The other problem with suffering is that it is essential to society evolving. Traits die off and fall out of favor and inevitably “suffer.” Under my beliefs, it is not possible. But under the assumption that a benevolent God does exist, we have a couple of possible outcomes.

First, if a benevolent God does exist, then he must not be capable of doing anything regarding suffering or creating it so that it is crucial to our survival. This is backward, as suffering should not exist in the first place under these conditions. The other possibility for the existence of suffering under the rule of a benevolent god would be that the benevolent god (good) has an equal and opposite good (bad) that controls aspects like suffering and peril.

Going into the details of the first possibility, being that benevolence and suffering coexist for some reason. If the benevolent god created suffering (or didn’t and happens to rule below it) it must exist for some reason. Either God doesn’t do anything about it or can’t because it must happen for the world to be the way it is. By the laws of physics, not everyone can be a winner, and things must oppose each other or balance each other out. Since this is true, good things must happen and so do bad things. Unfortunately, the unsettling nature of some of human’s best inventions or discoveries is the result of suffering. Think of the technology that resulted from war and conquest. Without suffering, these things may have never existed or even could have. Suffering also exists in “natural” scenarios, such as evolution, the cycle of life, and the food chain. With all of this, suffering must be consistent with the observable aspects of our world and can work under the existence of a benevolent god (because it is necessary for our world.)

Moving on to the second possibility, (which I believe is the more feasible of the two) where a god that is all-knowing and well coexists with a god that is responsible for all the suffering in the world. If a benevolent god exists and takes care of all gods in the world, then everyone should be happy. As I stated earlier, good things have to happen, and a benevolent god will give some form of good to everyone and everything in the world. But, since suffering does exist, a second God must be responsible for the opposite. Good cannot last forever, so suffering will take its place and be felt by everyone as well. A proper balance of good and evil may be necessary for the proper development of society. Also, the people who deal with more suffering than blessings just got bad luck. (Or if the gods are all-knowing, then they stopped a person from becoming something evil)

There is just as much evidence for the existence of one benevolent god as there is both a benevolent god and a god that controls suffering. In my opinion, both are unlikely, and suffering exists solely because it must. Humans begin their lives surrounded by pain, as their mother gives birth to them, and often die in pain or cause pain to those around them. It surrounds all of us, as does good and joy. Physics simply does not allow for the true coexistence of benevolence and suffering because of evolution and the natural laws that govern the world we live in every day.

prompt: morals… where do they come from, and are they valid?

To answer the first part of this question, we get our moral values from a few different main sources. The first and most prominent would be from our parents. The second would be from one’s culture/environment and the third would be a personal experience. Some other sources that could affect morality but less so would be one’s friends or occupation. Focusing on your parents/family as the root of your morals, we know that someone essentially gets every bit of knowledge about this world from their parents. Babies do not know how to be mean or nice, or on the extreme side, sexist, racist, and evil. Either morals are directly taught or are just picked up on from a parent. For example, my mother would teach me to always be thankful and show that I am grateful if someone else makes dinner for me or has me into their home. Someone that did not have this would likely not know to do such a thing or wouldn’t think to if society hadn’t conditioned this as normal behavior.

This leads me to the second way we get our morals, environment, and culture/society. While many things are taught to us by our parents or siblings, such as manners, language, and ideas, many more things are so because of the places we live and the way that those around us interact with them. The environment in the suburbs in America and interactions with other people is incredibly different than that of a country somewhere in Asia or Africa with much fewer resources per capita. For this reason, morals will be different from parents to children and from the environment to children because the conditions are much harsher. Morality is much less of a factor when people struggle for the necessities of life that people in much wealthier countries take for granted. Morals still exist in dire conditions, but what is learned from the environment will be much harsher outside of the United States than in the suburbs.

The third place to get morals would be through experience, which ties in with the environment. The environment dictates experience which also changes morals. Someone who witnessed a murder or faced tremendous hardship will likely have some moral changes after the fact as experiences like those are quite profound.

As for how we know how our morals are correct is a subjective debate. While I would argue that many humans across different cultures share some common morals regarding family, death, murder, and food, all morals are still man-made and are ultimately subjective. There is no true objective “right” or “wrong” because the answer will change depending on the viewpoint. Even if 99% of the people in a society agree about something, there is likely still someone somewhere who believes differently for their reasons, meaning that no moral can be 100% and absolute. As for our own beliefs, they can be validated by those around us or our interactions using our beliefs in the real world. A racist belief will likely be invalidated if he goes out and practices in public, whereas someone’s morals of just being a kind person and talking to others would likely fare well in this scenario. This is also only so because of how our society is set up. If this same scenario existed in a racist world, then those morals would be correct. This ultimately proves that morals are still subjective to society and just what group of people is looking at for that certain topic.
