field notes iv: july 7-22

Originally published: August 14, 2026

Maine <> Quebec <> Vermont <> New Hampshire <> Boston <> Providence <> NYC <> Philadelphia <> Washington, D.C.

I did not take many notes whilst with Mackenzie and Claire, or in Quebec, Boston, or Providence. So, I write the following in retrospect. NYC was the leg in which I took the most stream of consciousness notes, and you will detect the shift as it comes.

Portland, Maine and the surrounding lobster-farm-coastal towns all possessed a distinct feeling not quite like other coastal towns I have visited. For example, the geography is loosely that of the central west coast, but if Someone multiplied it laterally by a third. While rockier than I expected, indeed less dramatic, but still different enough to have allure or incite a distinct feeling. I presume the softer seasons are excellent here, and the winters harsh. I could sense ancient cryptids nearby, and the speed of it all in this NE region was a soft launch into what would become a swift and busy adventure. The people, kind and aging. The food, excellent (yet expensive, and yes, I chewed my oysters). The weather and land, welcoming. Lighthouses galore. Feels like old money. Probably is. The numerous Sotheby’s signs were telling.

Post-Maine, the journey north into Canada at the Jackman crossing caused me great anxiety! Why! Well, I had never been to Canada before! Or crossed a border in a rental van* before! Twas not notable in any sense, (aside from the fact that mackenzie directly disobeyed the border agents orders on pulling forward, and was incredibly short with us thereafter), and the most beautiful parts of driving on this entire leg occurred here. Once you are about an hour inland of the Maine coast, counties seemingly do not exist, and the “towns” are few and far between. The scenery gets much more dramatic, and logging trucks outnumber commoners, three to one. The firemen stared at us as we turned around, or passed their station twice. And with big eyes. waterfalls, mosquitos, and bits of French are detected in this strange region. Look at a map, what is going on up here? Not much, and I enjoyed it for that.

In Quebec, well, we stayed in “St-Michel-de-bellechasse”. Not Quebec proper, and I am BEYOND thankful for this! (apparently the name “Quebec city” is purely for map purposes or non-french speaking peoples of the modern day. In Quebec French, it is simply “Quebec” (keh-behck) While a 45~ minute drive to the “old city”, we never had to actually drive it. Thank god for the ferry service at “Levis”. Just two quick days in Quebec, but it was lovely nonetheless. Vastly different from Maine, as quite literally everything was in French. English was tolerated, but by no means welcome*. LOL. Mackenzie took four years of French in high school, and I have dabbled myself, so snuck by plenty fine. I suppose I was a bit shocked by how different the city felt from the American cities I have seen, and older ones at that. Much of old town Quebec and surrounding areas elicit a feeling of ancient class(?), or security, if that makes sense. A walled city, one of the only ones this far North in America to my understanding. Old town consumes one with the twisting alleyways, brick buildings, cathedrals, stairwells, dirtied grout, elevation, and so much more. I suppose this place is one of the closest things to Europe that you can get in North America. Old town Boston or Philly did NOT feel like Quebec at all. Food, shockingly cheap. Beer, also cheap. Cigarettes, ubiquitous and 65%+ of younger people smoke. (do not fact check me here.) Not so loud, dirty, or overwhelming. A beautiful and functionally modern city, outside of old town, built atop the cumbersome St. Lawrence River, and sticking out like a sore thumb as one enters those waterways. Oh, I can only imagine.. Lovely people and scenery, St. Michel was ANCIENT yet quaint, and a lovely place to bed down. Shoutout Nathalie, your grandmothers Sunday best was phenomenal, and I cannot stop thinking of it when I eat breakfast, which is rare. I am not going to attempt to make up something more novel here, but all I can say is that Quebec was a highlight, over the general NE region for me. Not traditionally Canadian in my mind. But TBH most americans should see Quebec at some point!

From Quebec, down thru Vermont and New Hampshire, staying one quick night in a river town along the way before a push to Boston. TLDR on this region; hippies, low population, few if any interstates (at least ones that felt like interstates), (yes!), nude beaches (isolated event, yes, it did happen, no I did not get naked, yes, I did see ****** n ***** n *******. Good for them!), glacial lakes (reminded me of Montana), scammy expensive border towns, second* best part of the entire drive (scenery wise), and a strange Airbnb/Hostel (shoutout DAVE and his special cats…)… The drive past Mt. Washington was short lived, but welcome, nonetheless. Strange part of the country. No billboards, as it is a law there.

Boston was lovely, and I enjoyed it more than I had expected, but I honestly have not much to say here either. I would return, the HI was welcoming and in a decent location. The north district was lively, but also had a high barrier to entry. Tourist and historical items, interesting, but the city has much more than just those locations. One thumbs up for Boston, not double.

Au revoir, Mackenzie. train to Providence, and a strange time there. I ended up walking nearly 30,000 steps during my half day in providence, and the smoke from the Canadian wildfires was smiting me. Ugh, carrying my 19.2 lb bag + backpack 1.6 miles both ways in 90+ degree heat and smoke. I welcome the challenge. I somehow did more in 8~ hours in Providence than I did during my second day in Boston.(?) My host was spiritual beyond all means I had previously understood, and I still feel touched by our conversation. :,) TBH not the best hostel experience but HEY! $35/n for a bed and a shower. World cup, triple burger, river walk, Mormon run in (always welcome, no I did not attend church in NYC, which is what they asked me), trader joes piss, brown uni, phone call with a friend, and so on.

~~~ en route home, jul 22 voice note, cleaned w/ spell check & grammar. ~~~

On trip number three, it's abundantly clear that I'm not seeking anything in particular. If anything, I'm just seeking to fill my present. not in a dopamine-slot-machine kind of way, but in a way that has no end, and I hope all future travel never has to end. I don't like fixing a point or purpose to something before I go there; it boxes it in and sets expectations. I can safely say that every city, state, or country I passed through on this trip came to me as I came to them. Novelty has a funny way of springing up on you when you don't expect anything. I am not finding myself. I am not discovering something new. And most great stories never have their Opus at the “end”. The strongest and most lasting moments occur in these medium moment in-between. I suppose I am discovering something NOT NEW here in my own way. Hmh.

I guess on the first trip or two I was more specifically sightseeing, but still very up in the air. I had no idea I was going to San Francisco or the Outer Banks, yet it still happened, it still came to me. I had some initial objectives, and it was no different on this trip, but even through all of them, I suppose I am finally using all the money I've saved in the last eight years or so of my life.

I guess the bigger thesis and proof I am living, is that I am not required to work the second I graduate. I am not required to even work in my line of work. I am not required to do what my friends or exes or parents think I ought to do. !!! And honestly, most of these people in my life don't have much of a vested interest one way or another. Being graduated imposes a newfound freedom, something I've been attempting to taste a little bit of over the last few years.

Most travel in the last five years of my adult life has been short sprints: either staying with a girlfriend and her family in a state far away, or family trips to islands or places out west. That type of travel was very pointed (social and group-focused).

The type of travel I've been doing this year is none of these. It's almost more of a way of life, which I guess, if I leaned into it harder, would be considered overlanding or van life. Part of me thinks I could do this and have a good time at it, but I feel like I've gotten 75% of what that life feels like in my ten weeks on the road this year so far.

And if I've learned anything, it's that I do love being able to come somewhere I would call home. I'm quite tolerant of being “transient-by-choice” and could do something like two months or longer away from home, mostly camping or in the car. It all makes me appreciate home more, but it also makes coming back feel less like something I'm dying to do.

I guess my younger self would be quite proud of me for actually embarking on these adventures. And I suppose my older self will be thankful for having had them, and for the doors they possibly opened that I'm unaware of even at this time.

If I had stayed exactly on track, I'd be working for a tech company here in Kansas City, probably still living at home and maybe planning to move out soon. None of this excites me, if anything, it sounds like hell. And I'm fortunate enough not to have had to do it.

However, I suppose I could phrase this phase of 2026 as a soft launch into “corporate adulthood”, but a bit more on my terms, that is, if I take a corporate job. However, half of the jobs I seek are not traditional nine-to-five salaries, so it's hard to say what this is at all.

But no, zero part of me feels behind, regressed, or like I'm making mistakes. As cliché as it is, Into the Wild by Jon Krakauer, the short nonfiction book about Christopher McCandless, did inspire me a lot when I was younger. Maybe not the aspects about completely abandoning everyone and ultimately meeting your own demise, because in the end I do think he was scared and missed his family.

One thing that is obviously true is that no amount of travel, new faces, new friends, or new relationships will give you newfound meaning or worldviews in life. I do think they have directionality, but I would caution those who travel as escapism against pursuing it as a means of running away from something. (Also advice to myself here!) And at least in my opinion, my mind is too hardened to let literal new places, people, or things truly make me forget all of the life I've lived thus far :,).

Another interesting thing about these trips: usually I miss last week, or last month, or the meal I shared with a friend four and a half months ago on a Tuesday, but oddly, I don't find myself missing many aspects of these trips, even this most recent one. Certain afternoons, evenings, or people I connected with, I do miss, but not in an all-consuming, overly nostalgic way.

I think part of this travel has been a rejection of being comfortable or complacent, especially in a world where it's easier than ever to stay put, listen to your echo chambers, and fail to feel uncomfortable in any way or see different cultures or people or landscapes. I find often that many revert, or bask in what is known. I urge young people to be uncomfortable, attempt not to regress or go back to the past. Why? What are you so afraid of? Why try something with a different filter in a familiar place that you know won’t last. I believe you are destined for more than that! I've talked to people who say they find no interest in this because they can just see it online. Or like staying put. Sure, I can hold the possibility that seeing or doing more outside of what is already known is a preference. But I think it is a shame. It is 2026… Perhaps a prediction for the future: as we grow ever more connected within our micro-niches online (or just hometowns, or where families are present), in some ways it's beautiful and brings us all closer, but I do think the people of today, and especially the children of tomorrow, will yearn for novel in-person interaction more. Whether this means Facebook groups or Discord servers start meeting up more, or if the young people of today rally in other ways (nothing new, just rediscovering I suppose) or whether there's some breakthrough on this front, I think something of the sort is coming.

Pre-internet, we had more universal cultural moments. Not only did news events bring people together, so did pop culture. Now I haven't even heard of most of the biggest shows or influencers that many speak of. But when I find somebody who is deeply into my interests the same way I am, it feels just like home. And few and far between are these people physically close to home.

I crave proximity to those I speak to, and I think that's what makes us human more than anything.

If anything, this most recent journey has made me feel like I really don't belong anywhere right now. And, oh, woe is me: twenty-three years old, seven months after graduating college, unable to find a job and just doing fuck-all. Of course, I feel dislocated. But I welcome this dislocation more than anything, and nothing excites me more about the future of my life than not knowing what the next three months, six months, one year, five years, ten years look like. That's not me advocating to become a heroin junkie… I'm just saying that absolutely nothing ties me down right now, and this sentiment is finally striking my core more than it did two months ago, more than it did five months ago, more than it did while I was at university. Over summer and winter breaks at university I yearned for this time, even then unaware of what it might look like.

I am totally unsure how long the travels may continue, or when the day comes that I finally pick a place and try to start playing real life again, because yes, my money would eventually run out if I just kept doing this forever. But while I have the energy and appetite for it, I might as well continue. I will say, my stamina has been decreasing. That's a signal that it's likely time to make some decisions. Welcome ones, at that.

I have mentally and physically closed out all of my loose ends from being a university student, and this adds to the free feeling. Thank god.

I recall walking past houses in my neighborhood, or any neighborhood, for that matter, especially the ones with the large front room, the vaulted ceilings, and the big drapes covering the windows against this hot sun. All I can think about is how kind it would be to share that space with somebody, lying on that warm carpeted floor or on the bed, or even on my lonesome, and forgetting everything else in the world.

That is what I will miss about Missouri. Space. Space that actually feels like mine. I love joining others in a park or square or on city streets, but the paths and roads I frequent at home truly do feel like mine. Not literally. I don't own those people's backyards or some trail I'm on, but when I'm the only one ever on it, it's hard not to feel some sort of ownership or belonging over it.

That is the weird and sad thing about times past. Intimate moments from precisely two years ago, or one year ago, can feel so at the front of my mind, yet in reality they are incredibly distant and never to be felt again. I think this part of me torments me, and I'm not sure how much of it is normal human emotion versus nostalgia or longing for something else.

So to the end of a NE speedrun. I will cherish the rats, no bathroom, the lobster and misty airs, or the Honda. Maybe I will even miss the noise of new York city, the speed, or the park. I guess I even will think on the smoky orange air of Providence and Boston, and think about days from four-hundred-years-ago. Oh America. I guess this is a love note to America, and being able.
