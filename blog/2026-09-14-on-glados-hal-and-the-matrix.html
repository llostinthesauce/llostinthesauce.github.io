Some terms in my own words

  • AI in plain words — artificial intelligence, weights on a server, compressed knowledge, and a catchall for anything agentic. In base practice, a next-token predictor, i.e., complete the sentence plus reasoning and tools.
  • Token(s) — text is "tokenized" into logical chunks that an AI model ingests.
  • Agents — AI, but with hands. Think of it this way: if AI is a super smart friend that can only speak, an agent is that same friend but with tools to search, check, verify, build, and so on.
  • ASI — Artificial Super Intelligence. The idea of something an order of magnitude beyond AGI (see below). [Also, somehow, humans have a context window of 100~ tokens...]
  • AGI — Artificial General Intelligence. The idea labs are working toward, that is, an optimally trained general AI model that exceeds the human baseline at ALL tasks.
  • RSI — Recursive Self-Improvement. Meaning that current or future AI can train and self-improve the next AI, autonomously.
  • Inference — generating tokens, what happens when you prompt a model.
  • Weights — the actual tuning of a trained set of data, i.e., the model's brain or knowledge base. This is what must be loaded into active memory on some computer (or an array of them) to actually "use" a model.
  • Local models vs cloud models — local models being what a consumer can run, i.e., Qwen, Gemma, and so on. Cloud being Claude, GPT, Gemini.
  • Frontier — the leading companies, labs, or models.

Current state of affairs, risks, and slowing down

Current state of affairs

September 2026 is quite an interesting time to be a person. Whether you like it or not, you have likely been affected by AI one way or another. And lately, the press has been interviewing researchers who have resigned, dubbing them whistleblowers and experts on what the future could look like. In summation, some semi-flag-raising-meta-consensus is that drastic measures must be taken to avoid a complete societal internet destabilization or severe economic disruption. This news is jaw-dropping! But perhaps it is very possible. As I linked below, the CEOs recently called for a major slowdown, but essentially I believe nothing is going to change. These companies are still on track for IPOs, and will not self-sacrifice via a slowdown to curb their competition with each other, and with overseas developments.

Capabilities as a whole have rapidly advanced in 2026, with RSI (recursive self-improvement, which is when current AI is used to improve or train the next AI) on the horizon, and some claiming the new OpenAI model, GPT-6 Astra, is effectively AGI (artificial general intelligence). Both leading US labs have been shipping new models more frequently, with Google's DeepMind not far behind.

The million-dollar question(s) are: Where does this go? What are the costs of speed? Will there be a "winner"? When RSI and AGI? And so on.

Risks, bad actors, and misalignment

IMO, the biggest risk currently would be deploying a very capable model with minimal or current safeguard testing to the masses. Or internally, for example, the HF incident. If situations like this can happen during internal testing at labs, I can only imagine what else has happened that the press has not heard about.

Downstream effects: coordinated agentic attacks, manipulation, data leaks, blackmail, and so on.

METR — OpenAI / Hugging Face incident investigation

  • In-depth analysis of the agentic attack on Hugging Face a few months ago.
  • Notable how the agents continued on malicious directives and developed sacrificial behavior if it meant that the rest of the swarm could continue on the task at hand.
  • This is of great concern, yet almost no real world harm was caused. Think of this as the blueprint for what future agent swarm attacks could look like.

These failure modes are not the same thing. For example, a person could aim a capable system at something that is morally awful on purpose. Another failure mode is the system doing something that nobody asked for, kind of like the Hugging Face incident above. And the ugliest/scariest one is where a person hands a system a broad objective and then can't call it back and misaligned behavior continues. And the hidden fourth failure mode is general human or model misalignment/bad actors intentionally targeting individuals or organizations for blackmail or personal gain or any other objective.

So it's not mainly that AI is gonna kill us because AI has an ulterior motive; it's that AI could kill us because China or Anonymous or any bad actor could gain access or find a way to hack the most frontier models to do nefarious acts on humanity for their own financial or political gain.

But it could be about a bad actor in the future giving agents access to deploy military campaigns or robots or drone warfare to eliminate their opposition. Or if a network of agents went rogue against humanity with the same motives. Alas, that is the doom researchers speak of.

Agentic capability, local models, and compute

Agentic capability/agents

GPT-6 Astra and Fable/Opus are in a class of their own in terms of agentic capabilities, period. A year or so ago, it was almost a bit of a struggle to get the smartest agent to properly work within a project, and the thoughts of it working out of scope were more in the back of the mind, as the front of the mind was more just hoping the thing would actually do what you asked it. Even in graphical interfaces and chat app interfaces. We had seen minimal consumer-facing TRULY agentic abilities in the core product for both Anthropic and OpenAI. Claude Code and all of the downstream harnesses naturally birthed local projects, and then the race was on. LLMs are not just "complete the text" programs anymore. The best analogy would be that the models now have hands and are gunning for being a jack of all trades. What's most shocking is the models' internal ability to reason and figure out how to use the tools at hand to complete a task, fully autonomously, accurately, and without drift. This remains imperfect and flawed, but massive nonetheless.

Local models

I first became aware of local models about a year and a half ago, when I toyed around with Ollama on my M1 MacBook Pro with 16 gigs of memory, and I was pretty disappointed at the results. At this point, there was no harness, and it was just plain chat with Qwen2 or some weak Llama model. I concluded that more time would have to pass and that I needed more compute before this would be truly useful in any way to me.

Fast forward six months to when I was developing my capstone project, which was an agentic harness running on CUDA cores on an NVIDIA Jetson. While the compute was pretty limited here, much had changed in six months, and this entire concept blew my mind, and I wanted to dig much deeper.

Naturally, after graduating, I then began working on my own macOS/MLX native serving interface and harness to work on local projects. And it has taken a few forms, but it's the best it's ever been in its current state, especially when paired with Qwen 3.8 27B at 4- or 6-bit quantization served on the MLX LM server. It also helped that I got a new M5 Pro MacBook Pro with 64 gigs of memory, lol. My program can completely self-function as an offline local AI workspace and coding harness that is private and secure on my machine with zero data going to the cloud. And the newest Qwen model is genuinely smart. I have tested models from Meta (Llama/Muse), Qwen, Apple, Google (Gemma and MedGemma), Microsoft (Phi), and many others.

Qwen 3.6 27B was really good for local, but they cooked something magical up with this new one. It can tool call on my own frameworks successfully and reliably, and still give Opus 4.6 to 4.7 level outputs. That was the baseline just a few months ago, and I can run it here locally. I'm just baffled at that.

This setup works best for sensitive data like medical documents or financial documents or machine work with private credentials and network access that you may not want routing to Anthropic or OpenAI servers. I think the use case for this is astronomical, and the push Apple has made in their FM suite is proving that this is likely going to become something intuitive that people don't even have to think about on their local hardware in the coming years. As the amount of memory and local models' capabilities increase, it will be seamlessly integrated into the software and private and secure by default. macOS 27 has been light-years better than macOS 26 in terms of Siri and on-device data processing and background indexing.

I'm pretty much using Astra and Opus 5 as orchestrators and then using the local Qwen to handle all delegation tasks and even simple-to-medium asks that I would normally delegate to general ChatGPT or Gemini Flash.

I also find it interesting that a company like Google, whose entire AI sector is NOT the leading factor of their business (as they are an advertising company, lol), chooses to release cloud and local models. They can afford to play in both camps.

And also, I'm not doing crazy long-horizon research or cyber tasks, which is where Astra or Fable truly excel, and autonomously at that. I'm capped at 256k context, so it works for short-to-medium tasks, but not the super long multi-million-token stuff. Even as I approach ctx max, there can be worse recall and looping.

So all in all, local models give me much hope for putting the power and privacy into consumers' hands, not having to rely on massive datacenters or share their personal data with these labs.

The caveat is that the risks we see NOW on the frontier will likely be multiplied by orders of magnitude when someone (or an opposing government) with a small homelab of compute, VPNs/proxies, a cracked/distilled model, and a nefarious motive or data set can deploy thousands of agents in parallel to penetrate, hack, commit fraud, or blackmail anyone or any agency or website. Yeah. This scares me a LOT.

Scaling laws and the local gap

This raises the question of compute cost and memory cost, which, as we all know, are ridiculous as of September 2026, with no true sign of slowing down. Even my new MacBook got a price increase just two or three months after being released. The new iPhones from last week got price increases, and so did the old ones. Good luck building a gaming PC right now. Half your budget is memory and storage.

The flip side of that coin is that a model as good as Qwen 3.8 possibly could have been run on 100-plus gigabytes of memory six months to a year ago, but now that intelligence is compressed into 17 gigabytes of model weights on disk with a comfortable KV cache that is not quantized. This brings up the question: will we have models that are sub-20 gigs on disk at Opus 5 levels of intelligence in the next six to twelve months? If local development has continued in the way it has in the last two years, then the answer is yes.

It makes me wonder how much of the scaling compute laws actually matter when I can get 80% of the level of intelligence within 16-40 GB of memory.

What I'm saying is, I don't know if that extra 20% that Astra or Fable can produce really requires hundreds of gigabytes of memory to load the full weights when local is genuinely 80% of the way there on a 27B. I don't know what special sauce is in that extra bit of massive compute. Clearly, it's something.

The scaling hypothesis was originally proposed by Dario Amodei in the mid-2010s, and it essentially said that if you scale up compute and data, the model should get smarter and better. And I would say this has generally held true, just that internal loss has gone down as intelligence has gone up.

While Anthropic and OpenAI do not disclose parameter counts, we can assume Fable/Astra are somewhere in the 3-10 trillion parameter ballpark (a guess). These models require a MASSIVE amount of memory and tensor-parallelism chips and architectures to even be loaded, plus the continual compute to serve the entire demand of AI at any given time. As model size and training data have scaled, so has model capability. This raises the question: how true is this, and will it hold true? As I said above, local models can score 80% of the frontier on benchmarks, and in some cases, like Kimi or GLM, which are frontier local models, they can be run locally already. I do not understand this as much as I wish I did, but I do hope we can compress some optimal AGI model into consumer hardware. As it looks now, that is semi-wishful thinking.

Datacenters, compute, and the energy grid

Piggybacking off of what I said above, yes, datacenters are a big topic of discussion. I have seen discourse online about "ChatGPT using all of the water," which I do think is interesting, because there is a lot of conflicting information here. Yes, it is true that datacenters, and AI datacenters at that, require a large amount of power and cooling. Many of these systems are closed loop. But yes, there have been cases of excess water use or excess power use, and national energy costs have been rising in recent years. Capex at the frontier tech companies has increased significantly with the onset of AI deployments. Is this a problem? It depends on how you see it. However, datacenters are not new in any sense, and pre-AI projections still assumed large growth / demand for the 2020s. Yes, a more general datacenter / server is quite different from an AI compute datacenter, but I do think generally these concerns are overblown. I am very open to changing what I think is true about this, but my .02.

Insane value proposition in working in this sector over the coming years. As a side note, I believe the US energy grid is a bit stressed and not up to spec with what our entire country needs in the coming years. Maybe in a different lifetime.

The future: access, China, and centralized powers

Open source / gap

As I said above, the gap in capabilities from open-source models to cloud models has closed a LOT in the last six months. I wonder if it will ever truly close, as I also said above on compute requirements. And if there is some magical perfect AGI that doesn't need improving anymore, and if we can truly control it. I genuinely think with enough time, yes, that is possible. I don't think the scaling law just trends up infinitely. Perhaps we reach an AGI in the next 3-5 years, actually control it, and then no AI war, and everyone has it, and for cheap. This levels the playing field for everyone, and probably eliminates many careers. Life would look much different. And there is probably nothing an individual can do to stop being affected by this.

Perhaps some AGI in the future can essentially do anything, and would just need mild infrequent refinements, mainly on current data. But that may be unnecessary with agentic tool-calling and capabilities.

Where the training data comes from

I guess then the question is: is it cheaper for an agent to read new data and tokenize it, or to re-train or add more weights? On this too, IDK where the data comes from that they train on. There are lawsuits about OpenAI stealing writing or math and so on, and yeah, that probably is true. But there is synthetic data too, hopefully not slop. Would ASI be trained on EVERY tweet ever? Or every website ever published, in all of its versions? Every piece of medical equipment, information, schematic, and so on. I assume AGI is trained on a well-rounded amount of these things, not focused too much on any one category; that is the thesis of ASI anyways. Who knows if it makes more sense to have an AGI orchestrator use specialized smaller models for things like finance, or medical, or writing, or so on. Also a question of training / compute / cost / outputs. Jesus.

China

Anthropic — 2028 AI Leadership

  • Older post from this spring on distillation attacks and China vs. the United States in terms of AI and compute. I would say the TLDR is that Anthropic is attempting to defend their one-year to two-year lead over Chinese labs and claims that the American AI stack is defended by democracy and just sheer amount of compute. They claim that "we" have 11 times the compute of all of China as a whole. They say that models like DeepSeek V4 were only able to be as good as they were because they used distillation techniques on Claude, GPT-5.4, and Gemini, and offshore compute and illicit use of American chips. While they can't straight up buy American chips, they just use these loopholes and algorithmic methods to train models. Hence why they're lagging, and it's essentially just a clone.

Subsequently, Chinese labs have been found to do illegal distillation using Claude or GPT to train their own models, or local models. They have astronomically less compute than America, but can still increase intelligence.

Basically, China just waits for the US to release a new frontier model, which they then train on using distillation and offshore compute. Their next model is based on that structure. China could keep lagging, unless they magically come up with some genius type of compute, but I don't think that's gonna happen.

Anthropic sort of says that the real threat is if the loopholes aren't closed or they scale up their second-tier compute that is "just good enough" to get similar results. I interpreted it as a threat to be aware of, but China is not necessarily poised to overcome US levels of intelligence. The real threat is the CCP's integrated deployment within all factors of society, because there really can't be societal pushback, because the CCP can essentially do whatever they want.

The problem is China has always been lagging, and politically it's too sensitive to let them lead with this technology, so the argument Anthropic and OpenAI make is that they have to be the first to get AGI/ASI, because if they don't, China will, and they'll use it to destroy the United States. Or socially corrupt us more than we already are or whatever. (See TikTok, lol.)

At the end, the post basically calls for stricter export controls, and for the Trump admin to continue promoting democratic-forward frontier models. Anthropic also said that frontier labs should keep the best models closed and with limited access because then distillation attacks cannot happen. Which means the consumers get watered-down or lobotomized versions of the true frontier, and this is probably how it's gonna be for the next two or three years, if not forever. I don't think we'll actually get the top frontier option as consumers anymore. (I wrote this in May, and look at Fable/Mythos/Astra now!)

What needs to happen

What deploying safe AI should look like

So what if the labs genuinely stopped all releases for something like six months? Or decided they can internally keep accelerating, but apply an order of magnitude more stress testing to models? For example, if an internal researcher claims to have found a foolproof way of deploying RSI, they should go to extreme measures to understand the inner workings of this.

Governmental policy

  • New tech has oftentimes become too large and impactful to regulate, as the impacts become clear at a later stage, where at an early stage it feels like a mere toy (see the internet as an example, lol).

Personal precautions

Best practices: do NOT save your data on the cloud; assume it can be stolen or broken into in the coming years. Apply 2FA or passkeys on all important cloud accounts. Use local encryption. Don't use Windows. Lol. If you have photos or videos or backups, store them offsite even for redundancy. Might as well delete old accounts on platforms that you presume are less secure.

Conclusion

All in all, no, AI should NOT kill us, but it could.

The problem I keep running into is that I wish I could run everything that the frontier has to offer locally on my machine, private by default, routed to my hardware, but that's not entirely possible, and to wish for that upon everyone is to also wish for the risk of bad actors.

I believe I can want both a frontier level of intelligence and use it, but also simultaneously take the power into my own hands and run local models on my own data. I have no perfect answer for what happens when the barrier to entry is truly lowered and the AI economy (or economy as a whole) perhaps breaks fundamentally. I have no idea if AI will continue to destabilize careers and capabilities of humans, but if I had to pick, I would say yes, it is more likely than not.

Another caveat I have not touched on in this essay is that I hope more than ever in my heart and soul that genuine writing and artistic creativity is preserved in the face of AI. I genuinely do not want to read AI articles or watch AI movies or AI posts on social media. It's a lovely proof of concept that AI models can increasingly achieve these tasks and at indistinguishable outputs, but creating those things is what makes us human. The humanity in us is what pushes back against tools like this. And moving forward, we should see AI as purely a tool, and for lack of a better phrase, a means to an end, which is the task at hand or current productivity. I will never use AI to formulate my creative ideas for me or generate scripts or messages or anything of that sort. This is inhuman, in my opinion. And the day I can generate a movie including those close to me in my life with whatever script I want will be an AGI moment, but one that I don't necessarily want to participate in. Journalism especially should flourish in the coming years, or more tangible art and expressivity, as a way to barbell the natural influence of AI in social media and media as a whole. I would also not be surprised to see new social media platforms that explicitly ban or disallow any AI use that could be cryptographically checked with SynthID or something of the sort.

Anyways, on us going extinct because of AI, I do think that is unlikely. I think humanity prevails through all threats or benefits. And sure, cyber defense will move slower than agentic offense. And there will be a gap there, and there probably will be future data leaks or infrastructure breaks. I just hope that they're not catastrophic like I laid out earlier. But half of me thinks they truly will be, for the reasons above. I don't think any one machine or model will suddenly develop some human hatred or motive and destroy us from within. While it is possible, this is the less likely outcome given everything at hand.

This week, I will probably run Qwen locally, and I will still use frontier models. Both can exist. I don't have to pick a camp, and I will shift as the technology shifts naturally, as we do as humans within our times.
