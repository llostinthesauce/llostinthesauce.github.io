Major Project A — K[REDACTED] Multi Studio Upgrade (A, C, Recording)

The existing state of the main K[REDACTED] Studios and office at the start of the summer was quite poor. The desktop Mac computers were from 2012–2013, and these are on 24/7/365. If the computer is offline, the station is offline. During my year as the student engineer, I constantly battled the computers and helped members get them back online. I pushed for the Curators to fund new computers, and they finally did! By July, we had three new desktop Macs, fully configured, and tons of new equipment, like monitors, a new sound board, and many other little upgrades. The objective was to get all of the old computers out, get the new ones online and enrolled with JAMF for security, set IPs, MAJOR cleanup/refinement, and get all of the software needed to make the studios top quality for Fall 2025. To start, we had to pull everything out and clean. This took 2–3 days on its own, and we did Studio C fist, the online online station, to troubleshoot. After C, we did A, which was a big overhaul. After that we did the Recording Studio and soundboard. The station was also filthy, so a full afternoon on its own was spent on just cleaning. [REDACTED] and I were in there until 4 AM one day. Time flies when you are having fun! The model of Mac that went into A and C was the M4 Studio with loads of RAM, 128GB. In the recording studio, we put a M4 Mac Pro Tower, for more I/O handling. In addition to the Macs, we needed new Audio in/out to the Macs, namely thru the use of a device called a Focusrite. The Focusrite takes I/O as ¼ jacks and is effectively a DAC. This gets sent to the Mac, is mixed there, then sent out to the Comrex, which sends to the tower. This is for A. For C, it is just Board -> Focusrite -> Mac. There were many hiccups in this progress across all the studios, because each input has to be configured, and CAT5 cable matched on the board, labeled, routed cleanly, then back to the Saffire, then to the Mac. There are about 30 “moving parts” for each board, so you can image the headache. We had to swap CAT5 Cables, run them thru the walls, and turn ports online for remote work on the Macs and FCC receiver units. Comrex is what sends the audio over network to the tower to be transmitted. We used dumb switches for network access and re-labeled everything. In the recording studio, we completely changed how the audio got to the Mac. We utilized a AoIP unit that has a GUI on the Mac to configure / route audio. This of it as a network-based audio hub/router. I learned loads here! We had to surplus all of the old equipment, as well as cables and board, and the 2013 Mac Pro “Trash Can” model. I got to buy one from the surplus lot! The new Macs got enrolled in JAMF after some help from IT, and it took a few days, but we got all of the software re-licensed and up and running. Prior to the upgrade, I made an extensive parts list, and post the upgrade, we needed a few extra cables or parts, which I also curated the list for. Following the upgrade, [REDACTED] had me create full topology diagrams for each of the studios so that the next engineer could have a great understanding of how everything gets to where/when. I also had the idea to run studio C and recording studio audio into the board on A, which [REDACTED] and I configured. This means that a live band could be in the recording studio playing and it could be sent to air on FM! Or used as a sort of communicative pass through for production, or just between studios.

Major Project B — Transmitter and Site Operations (K[REDACTED]/K[REDACTED]/[REDACTED])

I visited three main transmitter sites, and I will highlight three days in which some major work was done at each. First, at K[REDACTED], [REDACTED] and I had a full unit go out. At the transmitter, there are modular power units at something like 40W each or something of the sort, and one of the units failed. They company, Rhode and Schwartz, overnighted a new unit from Germany to be replaced. This entire process was wild, because we had to power down to take the units out. We took extreme caution to not touch the “hot” bits at the back of the transmitter, because it could result in a major electrocution. [REDACTED] let me do the main part of the swap and we ensured that it came back online. I also had full remote access to the transmitter, PDUs, and switches at the transmitter site which I used for daily/weekly logging of metrics. This consisted of me logging into the UM VPN via K[REDACTED]s channel and then using the IPs and passwords to login in a browser interface. I loved doing this. At K[REDACTED], [REDACTED] and I visited two main sites. The main site is shared with [REDACTED] for television. At the main site, [REDACTED] and I needed to setup a new PDU, power down the transmitter to complete the swap, and do routine cleanup as well. During this, we had to use arp -a to find the IP for the PDU, because it was configured earlier. We had some bugs on the backup transmitter, which I researched promptly, and diagnosed that there was a bad / faulty chip on the inside that handled GPS/time locking. At the classical site, we had the issue of a silence monitor failing or false alarming after 60 seconds. We found a unit that added dB in line, as to stop that from happening. We added it, and bang! The problem went away. The last site we spent a day at was in [REDACTED], where there is a student station, K[REDACTED], and an [REDACTED] affiliate like at [REDACTED]. It is a repeater for K[REDACTED]. On the day we were there, [REDACTED] had scheduled a tower climb, which is when a crew of contractors comes out and does a literal climb to check that the lines are good and the tower is safe. This was a bit scary to watch, but all was well. On the inside, [REDACTED] and I had to configure a PDU, and funnily enough, we had to use MS-DOS to do so. The switch was old and had never been brought online. We probably spend 3 hours scratching our heads trying to get it to work, but thru trial and error and both of us working, we realized that the port we were trying was set to the wrong VLAN, and it worked well after that. We also surplus-ed loads of equipment here, including old batteries and just dead rack mounted computers. Installation included routing cables, taking apart the line, splicing CAT5 cables, and more. The Orban audio processor was also acting up, so we would tweak settings on it, go to the station, change dB there, then listen back, and this troubleshooting continued until about 10PM and we got it sounding correct. We theorized that the tower climb had caused interference or something of the sort.
